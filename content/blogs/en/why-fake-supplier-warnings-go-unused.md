Buyers post bad reviews, complaints and warnings every day. But the information is spread across many places, most victims never report, and the platforms that could act on reports earn money from the sellers being reported. The result is a gap between what is known and what is done. This article explains that gap, using public data, and shows how a buyer can close it before paying.

## 1. The data exists, and there is a lot of it

Review data is huge.

- Trustpilot reported **301 million active reviews** at the end of 2024, with 61 million written that year.
- Amazon says it blocked **"hundreds of millions" of suspected fake reviews** in 2025.

Two things follow from this. First, there is plenty of information about sellers. Second, a lot of it is unreliable. Trustpilot removed **4.5 million fake reviews in 2024**, which it says is about **7.4%** of reviews submitted (up from 6.1% in 2023), and **90%** of those were removed automatically.

So a buyer who reads reviews is reading a mix of real experience and fake praise. That makes it harder, not easier, to see the truth.

## 2. Most victims never report

A warning only exists if someone writes it down. Most people don't.

- The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) estimated that Americans lost **$196 billion** to scammers in one year, but only **$12.5 billion** was actually reported for 2024.
- Law enforcement agencies believe that only **2% to 6.7%** of victims report their losses.
- In a 2025 AARP survey, about **41%** of adults said money or sensitive information had been stolen and used fraudulently, but only **21%** of those victims reported it to local police.

These numbers cover fraud in general, not only supplier fraud. But the pattern is the same: the complaint data that exists is a small part of what really happens. That means a supplier with only a few reports may still have many more victims who stayed silent.

## 3. The rules cover fake reviews, not how platforms handle sellers

Rules do exist. The FTC's rule on fake reviews took effect on **October 13, 2024**. It bans:

- Fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones
- Buying or selling reviews tied to positive or negative sentiment
- Undisclosed reviews from insiders
- Suppressing negative reviews through baseless threats or intimidation

Civil penalties can reach **$51,744 per violation**.

This is real progress. But notice what it targets: the businesses and people who create, buy or hide reviews. It does not decide how quickly a marketplace must remove a seller after buyers complain.

## 4. Why platforms can be slow to act

This section is my observation, based on how marketplaces are built. It is not a claim that any company ignores reports on purpose.

Many marketplaces earn money from sellers. A clear example is Alibaba.com. According to Alibaba's own seller guide, a free membership exists, but paid tiers cost about **$4,700 a year (Gold Supplier)** and **$12,500 a year (Verified Supplier)**, in return for more product showcases and better search ranking. (Prices vary by region and promotion.)

When sellers are customers, a platform has two interests that pull in different directions:

- It wants buyers to trust the marketplace.
- It also wants paying sellers to stay.

In practice, this often means a platform acts when a case is serious or when many reports arrive, not when a few buyers complain. A single report is easy to dismiss as one unhappy customer. That is a reasonable cautious approach for a platform. It is not a good approach for the buyer who is about to wire money.

To be fair, platforms also invest heavily in protection, such as the automated review removal described above, because a marketplace full of scams loses buyers. The point is not that platforms do nothing. It is that a buyer should not rely on the platform to have caught everything before they pay.

## 5. The data is scattered, and humans don't have time

Even when warnings exist, they are in different places:

- Reviews on the marketplace itself
- Reviews on third-party review sites
- Complaints on forums and social media
- Government and law-enforcement scam reports
- How old the supplier's website is
- The company's official business registration
- Whether the same phone number, address or email shows up elsewhere

To check all of these by hand, a buyer has to open many tabs, compare names, and judge what is real. It can take a long time, and when the order is urgent, most people skip it. That is not laziness so much as a lack of time. The information is available but it is not available **at the moment of decision**.

## 6. How a buyer can use the data anyway

You can close part of the gap yourself with a short routine before the first payment:

1. **Check the three names.** The name on the invoice, the name on the bank account and the name in the official business registry should match.
2. **Check how old the website is.** A very new domain is not proof of fraud, but it is a reason to look closer. You can use the free [Domain Age Checker](/tools/domain-age-checker).
3. **Search the phone number, address and email.** See whether they appear in complaints or on other, unrelated businesses.
4. **Read reviews for patterns, not scores.** Look for the same complaint repeated, and be careful with a flood of similar five-star reviews.
5. **Call the supplier on a number you already had** before you act on any change in payment details.

## The takeaway

The warnings are not missing. They are scattered, under-reported and slow to turn into action. Until that changes, the safest habit is to collect the evidence yourself, in one place, before you send money.
