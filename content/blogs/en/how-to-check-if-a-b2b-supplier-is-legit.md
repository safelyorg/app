Buying from a new supplier online is normal in B2B. Most suppliers are honest. But a fake supplier can look exactly like a real one: nice photos, a "verified" badge and a polite sales manager. The checks below help you tell the difference before your money moves.

You don't need to do all eight for a small test order. For a large order or a first deposit, do every one.

## 1. Check that the company is registered

Ask for the company's full legal name and registration number, then look it up in the official business register of its country. Many countries have a free online register.

What to look for:

- The **name** in the register matches the name on the listing and on the invoice.
- The **address** in the register matches the address the supplier gives you.
- The company is **active**, not closed or suspended.
- The **type of business** fits. A company registered as a travel agency should not be selling industrial pumps.

If the supplier will not give a registration number, or the number belongs to a different company, stop there.

## 2. Check how old the company is

A company founded this year is not a scam by default, but it has no history you can check. Scam suppliers often use new companies, because old ones would have complaints attached.

Look at the founding year in the register and compare it with what the supplier claims. "20 years of experience" from a company registered last year is a warning sign.

## 3. Check the contact details

A real company has contact details that point back to the same company:

- A **phone number** that works and is answered with the company's name.
- An **email address** on the company's own domain, not only a free email account.
- A **contact person** whose name and role make sense.

Be careful when the phone number on the listing is different from the one on the website, or when the email domain has nothing to do with the company name.

## 4. Check the website and its domain

Open the supplier's website and check:

- Does it show the **same company name, address and products** as the listing?
- How **old is the domain**? You can look this up with a free WHOIS tool. A website created a few weeks ago for a company that claims a long history does not add up.
- Is the domain **spelled like a famous brand** with one letter changed? That is a common trick.

## 5. Look closely at the listing

The listing itself often gives a fake supplier away:

- **The price is far below the market.** A real factory cannot sell much cheaper than everyone else for long.
- **Details are missing or vague.** No real minimum order, no delivery time, no port, or a price shown as a placeholder like "1234".
- **The photos are copied.** Search the product photos with an image search. If the same photos appear on many other sellers' pages, the supplier may not have the product.
- **The listing is in a regulated category** (for example medical, electrical or food products) and the seller is not the maker. Ask for the licences and certificates before ordering.

## 6. Watch how they want to be paid

This is the most important check. Most supplier scams end with a payment you cannot get back.

Be careful when a supplier:

- Asks for **100% payment up front**, especially on a first order.
- Pushes you to pay **outside the marketplace**, so its buyer protection no longer applies.
- Asks you to pay a **personal account**, or an account in a different name or country than the company.
- Suddenly sends **new bank details** by email ("our account has changed").
- Creates **urgency**: "the price is only valid today", "pay now to reserve the stock".

> **Tip:** When bank details change, do not reply to the email. Call the supplier on a number you already had, and wait before you send money.

## 7. Look for reports from other buyers

Search the company name together with words like "scam" or "fraud", and check trade forums and review sites. Remember that a new fake supplier may have no reports yet. No results is not proof that a supplier is safe.

Also check what the marketplace's **verification badge** really covers. On many platforms a badge means the company exists or paid for a membership. It is not a promise that the supplier will deliver.

## 8. Call them on a number you found yourself

Before a large order, call the supplier. Use a phone number from the official register or the company's own website, not the one in the email or chat. Ask simple questions about the order and the company. A real supplier will be happy to talk.

If you can, ask for a reference: another buyer you can call. For big orders, a factory visit or a third-party inspection before shipping is worth the cost.

## A quick checklist

| Check | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Registration | Name, number and address match | No number, or it belongs to another company |
| Company age | Matches what they claim | Founded this year, claims decades of history |
| Contact details | Same company everywhere | Different numbers, free email only |
| Website | Old domain, same details | New domain, or a brand name with a typo |
| Listing | Specific, realistic price | Far below market, vague, copied photos |
| Payment | Normal terms, on the platform | 100% up front, personal account, new bank details |
| Reports | Long, clean history | Complaints from other buyers |
