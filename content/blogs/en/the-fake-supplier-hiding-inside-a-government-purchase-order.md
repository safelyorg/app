$367,000.

That's how much the average company lost to invoice fraud, counting only the companies that actually lost money. The number comes from a survey of 800 procurement leaders in the US, UK, Germany and France, done this year. And 40% of them said invoice fraud had hit them in the last 12 months.

Now let me tell you about a scam that made the news earlier this month, because it shows really well how this kind of money disappears.

## It starts with good news

On 5 November, Malaysia's trade agency, MATRADE, put out a warning. People were pretending to be its officers and contacting businesses by email and WhatsApp.

And the message was the kind any small business would love to get. MATRADE needs your help, and it's urgent. Maybe with an overseas trade delegation. Maybe translation at an international exhibition. Maybe a company event.

Who says no to a government client?

## Then come the papers

Before any work starts, the "officer" asks for a few documents. Your company profile. Your registration certificate. Your bank statements. Your Ministry of Finance certificate.

Feels normal, right? Government clients always want paperwork.

But the scammers aren't checking you. They're collecting what they need to fake the deal. With your real documents in their hands, they make fake award letters and government purchase orders that look completely official.

## And then the real trap

So now you've got a purchase order with a government name on it. Here's the catch. Before the government pays you, you're told to buy some items first, like corporate gifts or special equipment, from a sub-vendor they "assign" to you.

You pay that sub-vendor up front. You expect the big government payment to cover it later.

That payment never comes. The sub-vendor was never a real supplier. MATRADE calls them "fraudulent shell accounts operated directly by the syndicate." In plain words, the scammers were on both sides of the deal. They were the buyer and the supplier at the same time.

MATRADE says several companies have already lost money this way. It didn't share a total.

## Why it works so well

Honestly, it's clever. And there's no hacking involved at all.

- **The order looks real.** It's built from the victim's own documents, so all the details match.
- **There's a rush.** The word "urgent" stops people from slowing down and asking questions.
- **Someone else picked the supplier.** This is the big one. When a client tells you who to buy from, it feels like checking them is their job, not yours. So nobody checks.

That last point is the one I keep thinking about. The whole scam depends on one supplier that nobody looked at.

## This isn't only a Malaysia problem

The story is local, but the pattern is everywhere.

In the US, the FBI received 24,768 complaints about business email compromise in 2025. That's the scam where someone pretends to be your boss, your client or your supplier over email to get paid. Together those complaints added up to $3.046 billion in losses. That's about $123,000 per complaint.

And back to that survey from the start. Invoice fraud took nine days on average to spot, and one in five companies needed two weeks or longer. That's a long time for money to be gone before anyone even notices. The same survey found that 19% of companies still check new suppliers by hand, with emails, documents and spreadsheets, and 6% have no steady way to check them at all.

## Five checks before you pay a supplier someone else chose

None of these take long. You just need to slow down for a few minutes.

1. **Confirm the order through a contact you find yourself.** Don't use the phone number or email in the message. Find the official website and call them. MATRADE says its real officers only use @matrade.gov.my email addresses, and never Gmail, Yahoo or personal WhatsApp for buying things.
2. **Be very careful with "pay first, get paid later."** MATRADE says it never asks vendors to pay a deposit or to pay a third-party sub-contractor's invoice. A real client doesn't need you to fund their order.
3. **Check the supplier like you picked them yourself.** Is the company really registered? Does the name on the invoice match the name on the bank account? How old is their website? You can check that last one with our free [Domain Age Checker](/tools/domain-age-checker).
4. **Search the supplier's phone number, email and address.** See if they show up in complaints, or on other businesses that have nothing to do with them.
5. **Think twice before sending your bank statements to someone you haven't checked.** Those papers are exactly what scammers need to fake a deal.

If you already paid, MATRADE's advice for Malaysia is to call the National Scam Response Centre on 997 straight away, file a police report, and keep everything, like your payment records, the fake documents and screenshots of the chats. Wherever you are, the same idea works. Report it fast and keep the proof.
